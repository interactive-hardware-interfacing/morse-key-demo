Parts

  • Seeed XIAO ESP32-C3 — one for wired, two for wireless — with the u.FL antenna that ships with it. Only the key board needs a breadboard.
  • A momentary pushbutton (any tactile switch works; a real straight key is more fun).
  • USB-C data cables (not charge-only) and, for the remote key, a USB power bank.

Things that bite

  • Swapped the boards? A board is whichever firmware it was last given. Reinstall from the Firmware tab; it takes a few seconds.
  • If you ever add wiring, avoid D0, D8 (SCK) and D9 (MISO) for anything tied to ground at power-up: on the ESP32-C3 they are boot-strapping pins.
  • Both wireless boards pin Wi-Fi channel 6 and need their antennas clipped on; range without an antenna is a few feet.
  • A board that won’t take firmware: hold BOOT (B), tap RESET (R), release BOOT, then try again.
  • If the chooser shows no port, the cable is the usual suspect — many USB-C cables are charge-only.

Pinout detail and a parts list are in wiring/wiring.md.

Put the firmware on the boards

Two roles, key and bridge, each a single code.py written straight from this page. Chrome or Edge.

1. Plug in

Connect one XIAO ESP32-C3 over USB. If the instrument is connected to a board, this page disconnects it first.

2. Pick its job

Install key for the board with the button (this one alone is the wired setup). Install bridge for the board that will stay at the laptop and receive. Pick the port named USB JTAG/serial debug unit.

3. Wait for the hello

The page writes code.py over CircuitPython’s REPL, checks its size and CRC on the board, reboots it, and waits for the firmware’s first line. Then do the other board.

4. Connect

Back on the instrument, click Connect bridge and pick the port of whichever board is at the laptop. Or skip all of this: tick Keyboard mode and use the spacebar.

Ready. Plug a board in and pick its role.

About this demo

What it shows, how to run it in front of a room, and how the decoding works.

What it shows

Before text messages there was Morse code: press a key short for a dot, long for a dash, and the gaps between presses separate the letters. Here you key it in and the computer decodes it live — and the same page works three ways, so it always has something to show.

  • Wired — a key on one board plugged into the laptop.
  • Wireless — key on a remote board, received by a bridge across the room.
  • Keyboard — no hardware at all: hold the spacebar. Great as a backup if a board dies mid-tour.

Try this during the tour: tick Keyboard mode and tap · · ·　— — —　· · · (SOS). Then hand a student the real key and have them send their initials.

How it works

The board reports clean key edges — “down at t”, “up at t” — over USB. All the timing lives in this page: a press shorter than about two dits is a dot, longer is a dash; a gap of about three dits ends a letter, seven ends a word. The dit length slider sets the pace. Keyboard mode feeds the exact same decoder from your spacebar. The edge line format is in PROTOCOL.md.

Known quirks:

  • Decoding depends on rhythm. If letters run together, raise the dit length; if everything reads as dashes, lower it.
  • Audio only starts after you tick the Audio box — browsers won’t play sound before a click.
